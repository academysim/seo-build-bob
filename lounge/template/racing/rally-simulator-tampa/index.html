On A Simulator

Rally is run against the clock rather than wheel to wheel. One car at a time, on closed public roads, with a surface that changes under you from corner to corner. It asks for a different kind of car control from circuit racing, because the car spends far more of the time slightly out of shape.

Why Rally Is A Different Problem

On a circuit you learn a fixed piece of road and refine it over many laps. On a stage you usually get one run, and even after a reconnaissance pass you are working from notes rather than from repetition.

That changes how you drive. Circuit racing rewards building a precise mental model of a known corner. Rally leans more on reading the road as it appears, leaving margin for what you cannot see, and correcting a car that is already sliding rather than avoiding the slide in the first place.

The surface is the other half. Gravel, dirt, snow and tarmac all behave differently, and a loose surface changes as cars run over it, cutting ruts and pulling clean gravel onto the line. Grip is never quite what it was on the previous corner.

Pace Notes

In rallying a co-driver reads a running description of the road aloud, ahead of the car, throughout the run. Drivers may recall parts of a stage they have run before, but the notes are what they drive to.

Notes are typically written by the crew during a reconnaissance pass, using a shorthand for corner severity along with markers for crests, jumps, surface changes and hazards. Systems vary between crews, and some grade corners by number while others describe them in words. Either way the driver commits to a corner they cannot yet see on the strength of what they have just been told, which is why the trust between driver and co-driver is talked about the way it is.

Learning to act on a call rather than on what your eyes have already confirmed is one of the more unusual things about trying rally, and it is the part people tend to remember.

The Techniques That Show Up

Weight transfer

Deliberately shifting load onto the front tires before turning in, so the car rotates. On loose surfaces this matters far more than on tarmac, because there is less grip available to steer with.

Left-foot braking

Braking with the left foot while still on the throttle, to settle the car or adjust its attitude without unloading the drivetrain. Common in rally and awkward to learn at first.

Handbrake turns

For very tight hairpins, locking the rear wheels briefly to rotate the car. Four of our rigs have a handbrake, so this is one of the few places you can practice it properly.

Looking far ahead

On a stage your eyes need to be a long way down the road, because you are reading rather than repeating. It is one of the habits that differs most from circuit driving.

Which Rig Suits It

Rally is one of the categories where the hardware changes what you can practice, because of the handbrake.

Four of our rigs carry a six-speed H-pattern shifter and a handbrake, with load-cell or active pedals. Mention rally when you book so one can be set aside where possible. Direct drive steering matters here too: on a loose surface the front wheels are constantly being deflected, and the wheel reports that back to you.

Motion platform

Our flagship is a Trak Racer TR160 V5 on four D-BOX Gen 5 actuators, giving pitch, roll and heave, with triple screens and a real cockpit. Crests, compressions and surface changes register physically as well as visually.

H-pattern and handbrake rigs

Four rigs are fitted for shifting through a gate and using a handbrake, which covers the techniques rally actually asks for.
