What A Prototype Is

A prototype is a racing car built from scratch to a set of rules, rather than developed from a road-going model. Nothing about it has to relate to a car you could buy. Current top-flight prototypes are usually closed-cockpit, though open-cockpit prototypes have raced for much of the category history.

That freedom shows up mainly in aerodynamics. Prototypes typically generate more downforce for their weight than GT cars, and a large share of it comes from the underfloor, which is shaped to accelerate air beneath the car and reduce the pressure there. Aerodynamic grip rises with speed, though how it builds depends on ride height, aero balance and the specific car, so it is not a simple linear relationship.

They are also built for the specific problem of running a very long way. Driver changes, refuelling, quick-change bodywork, lighting for night running and cooling that survives hours at racing speed are all designed in from the start.

The Thing That Makes It Different: Traffic

Most endurance racing is multi-class. Prototypes and GT cars share the same circuit at the same time, with a substantial speed difference between them, and both are racing their own class rivals while doing it.

That produces a skill particular to the category. A prototype driver plans a pass on a slower car ahead of time, ideally without compromising their own line or the GT driver’s race. A GT driver is expected to be predictable and not to surprise the faster car arriving behind them.

It is a different mental exercise from a single-class sprint, and it is a large part of what draws people to the category.

What It Asks Of A Driver

  1. Consistency over one fast lap

    Running within a small window of your own best time, stint after stint, is what teams tend to value. Consistency is discussed in endurance circles at least as much as outright pace.

  2. Managing what you are given

    Tires degrade, fuel load changes the handling as it burns off, and brakes have to last. Driving to preserve the car is a skill in its own right, and one that barely features in a short sprint.

  3. Awareness of everything around you

    Mirrors and spatial awareness matter more when a much quicker car is closing on you. Knowing where you are on the circuit, who is behind, and what class they are in becomes constant background work.

  4. Discipline when it stops being fun

    Long stints are physically and mentally tiring, and holding concentration when the novelty has worn off is part of what the discipline asks for.

Which Rig Suits It

Long stints reward a comfortable, stable driving position more than anything else, so the rigs with a fixed racing seat and a wide field of view suit endurance best.

Motion platform

Our flagship is a Trak Racer TR160 V5 on four D-BOX Gen 5 actuators, giving pitch, roll and heave, with triple screens, direct drive steering, active pedals and a real cockpit. The platform adds physical cues alongside what the wheel and screens are telling you.

Triple screen

Peripheral vision matters a great deal in multi-class racing, because a large part of the job is knowing what is beside you. Triple screens widen the field of view considerably.
