What Makes A Formula Car Different

The defining features are exposed wheels, a single central seat, and a chassis built around the driver rather than adapted from a road car. Everything else follows from those choices.

The wings are there primarily to generate downforce, pressing the car onto the road so it can corner harder than tire grip alone would allow. In modern single-seaters a large share of that downforce comes from the underfloor rather than the wings, and managing the turbulent air thrown off exposed rotating wheels is a significant design problem in its own right. The bodywork between the wheels is narrow, so the driver sits low, reclined and close to the front axle, with a view down the road very different from a closed cockpit.

Mass is the other half of it, though the range is wide. Junior single-seaters can be well under half the weight of a GT car, while a 2026 Formula 1 car is around 800 kg with a wheelbase over three meters. What is consistent is that open-wheel cars carry less mass for the grip available to them than closed-cockpit racing cars do, and mass is what makes a vehicle reluctant to change direction.

Open-wheel racing spans a very wide range, from entry-level regional classes to the fastest cars in the world. Characteristics differ enormously between them. Academy Sim Racing is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or licensed by any racing series, sanctioning body or car manufacturer.

Why It Is Unforgiving

Usually no driver aids

Most open-wheel classes run without anti-lock braking or traction control. A locked front wheel stays locked and a spinning rear stays spinning until you correct it yourself.

Downforce needs speed

Aerodynamic grip builds with velocity, so the car has far less of it in slow corners than in fast ones. Carrying a fast-corner mindset into a hairpin does not work.

Responsive to steering

In the lighter classes especially, the car changes direction readily. That rewards precise inputs and punishes hurried ones, which is why smoothness is emphasised so heavily in open-wheel coaching.

Exposed wheels

In real racing, wheel-to-wheel contact between open-wheel cars is far more consequential than between closed-wheel cars. That shapes how these classes are raced, and it is worth understanding even in a simulator.

None of this makes open-wheel a bad place to spend an hour. It does mean most people enjoy it more after some seat time in something with driver aids, which is why the GT3 page exists.

Which Rig Suits It

Our library runs iRacing and Assetto Corsa, covering more than 170 cars across more than 140 tracks, and includes open-wheel machinery at several levels.

Motion platform

Our flagship is a Trak Racer TR160 V5 on four D-BOX Gen 5 actuators, giving pitch, roll and heave, paired with triple screens, direct drive steering, active pedals and a real cockpit. The platform supplies motion cues as the car loads and unloads, alongside what the wheel is telling you.

Triple screen

A wide field of view matters here. Open-wheel cars place the front wheels in your peripheral vision, and triple screens put apexes roughly where your eyes expect them.

Single-screen rigs are also available. Direct drive steering means the wheel loads up as the front tires take load, which is one of the clearest sources of information about what the front of the car is doing.
