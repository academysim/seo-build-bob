Why Learn A Track In Advance

The first session of a track day is largely spent working out where the circuit goes. Simulator practice can cut down the time it takes to learn the corner sequence.

What transfers well is layout and sequence: which way the next corner goes, whether it tightens, what follows it, where the road falls away, and which corners matter because they lead onto a long straight. Knowing that in advance frees your attention for the things that need it in the moment, which are your instructor, the flags, the mirrors, and what the car is telling you.

What does not transfer is the feel of the real car, the grip of the real surface on the day, or your own braking references. Those you build on site, and an instructor sets them with you.

Simulator time builds circuit familiarity. It is preparation and practice, not a substitute for real-world instruction, and braking points from a simulator should not be carried directly onto a real circuit.

How To Use A Simulator Before A Track Day

  1. Learn the sequence, not the lap time

    The goal is to know what comes next without thinking about it. Chasing a fast lap in the simulator is a different exercise and a less useful one if you have an event coming up.

  2. Run the car you will actually drive, or something near it

    A circuit feels different in a car with downforce than in one without. Getting closer to your real car makes the layout knowledge more transferable.

  3. Pay attention to elevation

    Maps flatten circuits. A crest that hides an apex or a corner that falls away is much easier to understand once you have driven it, even in a simulator.

  4. Leave your braking references at the door

    Learn where the corners are, not where to brake. Real references depend on the car, the tires, the conditions and you, and your instructor will set them on the day.
