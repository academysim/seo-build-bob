Daytona is really two circuits sharing one site. A flat infield section that rewards patience, and banking steep enough to load the car in a way nothing on a normal road course does. Knowing which part you are on, and what changes between them, is most of the job.

Why Daytona Is Different

The speedway opened in 1959 as a 2.5 mile oval with 31 degrees of banking in the corners and 18 degrees across the tri-oval. The road course used for sports car racing is 3.56 miles with 12 turns, and it is built from two very different kinds of road.

The road course leaves the tri-oval for a flat infield section, rejoins the oval, runs the backstretch with a chicane partway along it, and finishes the lap through the banked corners at the far end of the speedway. So a lap combines slow, technical infield driving with long high-speed running on the banking.

That split is what makes Daytona unusual to learn. You have to adjust between the flat infield and the banked oval sections, and the transitions between the two are where the car changes most.

The oval was completely reconstructed in 2010. The previous resurfacing, in 1978, was an asphalt overlay.

The Corners That Matter Most

Infield Entry

Where the banking stops

The road course leaves the tri-oval and drops into the flat infield through a right then left complex. The car arrives carrying speed from the banked tri-oval onto flat pavement, so arriving too fast here compromises the whole sequence that follows.

International Horseshoe

One of the slowest corners on the circuit

A long right-hand hairpin and one of the slowest parts of the circuit. It is long enough that an early turn-in leaves you short of road at the exit. It is followed by more infield rather than a straight, so the exit matters less here than it does at the corners that feed the oval.

The Kink and West Horseshoe

The run back toward the oval

The Kink is a quicker left that leads into the West Horseshoe, another right-hander. These corners set up the approach to the left that takes you back onto the banking, so they affect how much speed you carry into the oval section.

Rejoining The Banking

The biggest transition on the lap

A left-hander that feeds the car off the flat infield and back onto the banking. This is the biggest transition on the circuit. The infield is flat and the oval is banked, and the camber change through the rejoin is one of the things drivers notice first at Daytona. The exit sets your speed for the run down the backstretch.

Le Mans Chicane

The one that used to be the Bus Stop

The chicane partway along the backstretch, known for years as the Bus Stop and renamed the Le Mans Chicane in 2022. Its four changes of direction run left, right, right, left, so the first input on approach is a left. It interrupts the fastest part of the lap, so its braking zone can offer an overtaking opportunity, and an overcommitted entry compromises the rest of the sequence.

The Banked Oval Corners

Thirty-one degrees of banking

The lap finishes through the banked corners at the far end of the speedway, at 31 degrees. Banking tilts the road so that part of the force supporting the car also pushes it into the corner, which means more speed is available at a given radius than on a flat corner. For a driver used only to flat circuits it is a genuinely different sensation. A simulator shows the visual change clearly, though how the steering feels depends on the software, the car and the hardware.

Corner numbering at Daytona depends on which layout you are running. The sports car road course is 3.560 miles with 12 turns, while the road course NASCAR uses is 3.570 miles with 14 turns, so the same corner can carry different numbers. We have used corner names here for that reason. Check the numbering on the map your organizer supplies.

Who Runs Events At Daytona

Daytona is not an open lapping facility. Amateur and HPDE access comes through organizations that rent the circuit for scheduled events, so you sign up with the organizer rather than with the track.

Two we can confirm currently run events on the road course are Chin Track Days and SCCA Track Night in America. Dates and run group structures change from season to season, so check the organizer directly rather than relying on a summary like this one.

We are not affiliated with Daytona International Speedway or with any of the organizations named on this page. Check current schedules, entry requirements and technical rules with the organizer before you enter.

How To Get The Most From Your First Track Day

  1. Learn the layout before you arrive

    Daytona is a circuit where knowing the sequence matters more than usual, because the infield corners come quickly and look alike to someone seeing them for the first time. Learning which horseshoe is which in advance means your first session is about driving rather than orientation.

  2. Respect the transitions, not just the corners

    The parts of Daytona that surprise people are the changes between flat and banked surfaces rather than any single corner. Learn where those transitions are, then work on the line with your instructor once you are there.

  3. Be honest in your run group placement

    Entering a group above your experience is the fastest way to have a bad day. Organizers place drivers by experience for good reason, and a novice group at a circuit this quick is not a soft option.

  4. Use your instructor properly

    Tell them what you want to work on and ask questions between sessions. An instructor who knows what you are trying to fix is far more useful than one who is guessing.

  5. Prep the car and yourself

    Have the car inspected and follow the organizer’s requirements for brakes, tires, fluids and cold tire pressures, and clear anything loose out of the cabin. Summer events in Florida get hot and humid, so follow the organizer’s guidance on hydration and heat.

  6. Build up rather than starting at ten tenths

    Leave margin, especially on the banking, where speeds are highest and the consequences of a mistake are largest. Build pace as conditions and your instructor allow.

Simulator practice builds circuit familiarity. It is preparation, not a substitute for real-world instruction, and braking points from a simulator should not be carried directly onto a real circuit.
